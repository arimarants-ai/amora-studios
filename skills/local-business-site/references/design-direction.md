# Design direction

Read this before writing any CSS.

Everything else in this skill is fixed on purpose. This part is not, and the
quality of the site depends almost entirely on how seriously you take it.

## Where a direction comes from

Not from a palette generator, and not from what looked good last time. From the
business's own world — the materials it works with, the room it happens in, the
instruments it uses, the words its customers use.

Spend two minutes on this before touching a colour:

- **What does the place physically look like?** A tattoo studio, a dental
  practice and a bakery do not share a visual world, and a site that ignores that
  is a stock template with a logo on it.
- **What is the customer feeling when they arrive?** Someone booking a funeral
  director and someone booking a padel court want opposite things from a page.
  Calm and reassuring, or fast and energetic, are different designs, not
  different colours.
- **What is the one thing this business has that its competitors do not?** That
  is usually the hero, and often the whole structure.
- **What would embarrass the owner?** A barrister does not want playful. A dog
  daycare does not want austere.

Write the answers down. The direction falls out of them.

## Deciding the direction

Commit to these four before you build, in writing:

**The ground.** What the page is made of. Not "white with an accent" by default —
consider a deep single-colour ground, a paper texture, a photographic hero, a
high-contrast editorial black and white, a colour field, a duotone. The ground
decides more of the character than the accent does.

**The type.** Two faces, three at most, chosen for this business. Typography
carries more personality than colour and is the first thing that makes two sites
look related when it repeats. A grotesque, a geometric sans, a slab, a
transitional serif, a display face used only at size, a monospace used
structurally — these produce completely different pages.

**The structural idea.** How the content is organised on screen: a strict grid, a
single narrow column, an asymmetric split, full-bleed alternating bands, a
sidebar that persists, cards, a table, an index. This is where most
template-sameness lives, because it is the part people copy without noticing.

**The one distinctive move.** One thing this site does that the last one did not.
An oversized type treatment, a real photographic identity, a colour that should
not work, an unusual nav, a genuinely useful interactive element, a considered
empty state. One. Spend the boldness in a single place and keep everything around
it quiet.

If that written direction could be pasted onto an unrelated business without
edits, it is not a direction. Redo it.

## Looks to avoid

These are not banned, they are exhausted. If you land on one by default rather
than by argument, you have not designed anything.

**The one this skill's author already used.** Warm off-white paper, near-black
ink, a single terracotta or rust accent, a high-contrast serif display against a
neutral sans, full-width bands alternating light and dark, soft shadows, cards
with generous padding that lift on hover. It is a good look. It is also the look
of the site this skill was extracted from, and reproducing it is the specific
failure this file exists to prevent.

**The current AI-design house style.** Cream backgrounds with a serif and a
terracotta accent. Near-black with one acid-green or vermilion pop. A purple to
blue gradient hero on white. Inter or Space Grotesk as the safe choice. Emoji as
section markers. Everything centred. Rounded corners on everything. An accent bar
on the left edge of a card. Numbered eyebrows — 01, 02, 03 — on content that is
not actually a sequence.

**Every-startup layout.** Hero with a centred headline and two buttons, then a
three-up feature grid with icons, then a logo strip, then a testimonial carousel,
then a repeated call to action. It is not wrong, it is invisible.

## Forcing variety

When several sites get built with this skill, sameness creeps in through the
axes nobody thinks about. Deliberately vary:

| Axis | Options |
| --- | --- |
| Ground | light, dark, coloured, photographic, textured |
| Type pairing | serif+sans, sans+sans, mono accents, one family across weights |
| Nav | top bar, side rail, minimal with anchors, bottom bar on mobile, none |
| Section rhythm | alternating bands, one continuous ground, boxed panels, full-bleed images |
| Density | airy and editorial, or dense and practical |
| Imagery | photography, illustration, iconography, type only, pattern |
| Corners and edges | sharp, soft, mixed, hairline rules, no borders at all |

Look at what you did last time and pick differently on at least half of them.

## Plain is allowed

Distinctive does not mean decorated. A plumber's site that loads instantly, puts
the phone number in the top right, and says what areas they cover is a better
site than an art-directed one that buries the number. Density, restraint and
speed are design choices too.

The test is not "is it striking", it is "does this look like it was made for this
business and nobody else".

## When they give you direction

Their words win. Every time, including when they ask for something on the avoid
list. If a client wants cream and terracotta, build cream and terracotta and
build it well. This file governs what you do when nobody has said.

If they show you a reference site, take the *principle* from it — the density,
the type contrast, the way it handles images — rather than copying its layout.
